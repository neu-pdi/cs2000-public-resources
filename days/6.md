### Why Value-Sensitive Design?

Computer science is a group activity. In this course, we are teaching the
vocabulary for moral language, so that you can talk with your teammates and
advocate for what you think is right. In particular, we focus on identifying and
defending privacy issues, to push back on the common excuse of "I have nothing
to hide", using a process called _Value-Sensitive Design_. As part of it, we
build _stakeholder-value matrices_, which help us realize which people might be
put at risk because they _do_ "have something to hide."

These steps happen at the very beginning of designing a program, when we are
figuring out its requirements: they help shape what a program should do, before
we dive into the code and data. Two examples show why this is useful:

- Social media apps were built to connect communities and bring users closer
  together. But the social media company itself is also a stakeholder, with
  conflicting values: it makes more money when users look at its content (and
  advertisements) for longer. Those financial values can lead it to design the
  platform to keep users looking at the screen, which can conflict with the users'
  mental health.
- reCAPTCHA and Duolingo were built to verify that users are human and to teach
  new languages, respectively, but both also use the data their users generate to
  train AI models. And like social media, Duolingo's financial values push it to
  make users open the app more often, which can mean prioritizing viewing over
  learning.

### Steps for Value-Sensitive Design

1. **Identify the stakeholders**: the people who are affected by the software, in any way.
2. **Identify the values**: the values at stake for those stakeholders, when considering the software.
3. **Fill out a stakeholder-value matrix**: a table whose columns are the values and whose rows are the stakeholders, where each cell says how that stakeholder's value relates to the software.
4. **Identify and analyze conflicts in the matrix**: for each conflict in the matrix, identify whether and how to address it.

### Case study: Finder, a dating app

Consider "Finder," a fictitious dating app.

**Step 1: stakeholders.** Users are only a few of the many people affected by a
technology, and even "users" contains many different groups: nonbinary people,
transgender people, people who date people of all genders, people whose sexual
orientation is criminalized where they live, people who are not legally allowed
to be where they live, sex workers, survivors of intimate partner violence, people
who are blind, people who don't speak English, people whose names have
apostrophes in them, and many more. Beyond users, there are the **content
moderators** (who check for disallowed content, and so are exposed to harmful
content), the **software engineers** and other **employees** of the company,
**advertisers** and their other customers, the **actors and influencers** in the
ads, **governments and regulators**, and **anyone who dates**, since dating apps
have changed the way everyone dates -- along with the friends and families of
people in relationships formed on the app. More stakeholders will appear over time.

**Step 2: values.** Values shape technology, whether or not the people building
it acknowledge them, and once built, technology can change our practices and
values in turn. **Privacy** is a key value throughout this course: many users
will value it, whether to hide their identity from a government or their
location from an abusive person (**safety** can be listed separately, or as part
of privacy). **Accessibility** means different things for different users:
nonbinary people need a gender option that includes them; people who are blind,
or don't speak English, need Finder to work with screen readers and translation
apps; everyone needs to be able to enter their actual name. **Fairness** only
happens if we prioritize it: recommendations should take into account users'
own preferences, and nothing else. **Financial cost** matters to advertisers and
users, **reputation** is valued by many, and many people value **laws and
regulations**.

**Step 3: the matrix.** Here is part of it (the full matrix has a row for every
stakeholder, and a column for every value):

| | Privacy | Accessibility | Financial cost | Laws and regulations |
| -- | -- | -- | -- | -- |
| Nonbinary people | May want to keep gender identity hidden | Need a nonbinary option for their profile | May want to keep costs reasonable | Generally want to exist legally |
| People whose sexual orientation is criminalized where they live | Likely want to keep their sexual orientation hidden from the government | Need to search profiles of appropriate genders from their location | | |
| Survivors of intimate partner violence | Likely want to keep their location hidden from certain people | Need two-factor authentication, or other ways to prevent account takeover | | Hope we go beyond the minimum regulations for protection |
| Content moderators | Want to keep their identities hidden from users | Need access to everything they need, and nothing more (no personal data or direct messages) | Need to be well-compensated for seeing harmful content | Must follow laws about allowed speech and personal data |
| Advertisers | Have some data to share, and some to keep private | Want users' contact information, but shouldn't get it | Want to reach more people for less money | Must follow advertising regulations |
| Governments and regulators | Want to keep their identities hidden from users | Need access to everything they need, and nothing more | Need to collect accurate taxes | Must enforce the law |

**Step 4: conflicts.** Some stakeholders' values conflict. Advertisers want to
keep their costs low, but that may push costs onto users, or onto the company:
choosing a financial model means choosing between them. Many users want to keep
their identities or locations private from the government, while the government
may want that information to enforce laws; a mitigation might be to keep all
information private until regulators provide a subpoena, or to only ever provide
specific information (not location or sexual orientation). Fairness and
accessibility can sometimes risk Finder's reputation, depending on who is giving
opinions, and ensuring fairness can require collecting personal information,
which can compromise privacy. And there is often a conflict when criminalized
groups simply want to exist legally and peacefully, despite being targeted by
governments.

### Throughout the Process: Bias and Unfairness

Bias and unfairness are so built into our society that counteracting them is a
challenging, continuous task, which doesn't end with a stakeholder-value
matrix. But there are some concrete places to watch for them. They can enter our
technology in many ways:

- The technology might rely on a dataset that doesn't accurately reflect the
  population it represents: for example, a dataset of "the general population"
  that leaves out women.
- The tools used to collect the data might be biased: for example, a survey
  with multiple-choice questions where no choice applies to some people.
- The people building the technology may misunderstand the data (not
  understanding the context in which it was collected, say), or make mistakes,
  such as taking a correlation between variables to mean one causes the other.
- The technology might be used for a different purpose than the one it was
  built for: for example, a platform built for vital communication in
  vulnerable communities used to spam and harass those same people.

And bias and unfairness can be created or amplified once the technology is in
use. Technology reflects historical injustices as they unfold and compound (for
example, the numbers computers use to represent words reflect the biases of
the text they were computed from); it can magnify the impact of its users'
implicit biases, and reinforce their stereotypes; a tool that is very useful to
one population may be harmful or inaccessible to another; and unfairness
compounds through feedback loops, as when social media highlights posts that
already have a lot of attention, giving them even more.

### Privacy

Privacy is "the ability to determine for ourselves when, how, and to what extent
information about us is communicated to others" (Westin, 1967). But individual
privacy often appears to conflict with the interests of society or the state,
as in the supposed "tradeoff" between privacy and national security, and if
privacy is _only_ understood that way, privacy often loses. So decisions about
privacy are often made by looking at the social context, to help us determine
the right tradeoffs. Throughout this course, we use these questions to help make
those decisions:

| Question | Answer |
| - | - |
| What type of information is shared? |  |
| Who is the subject of the information? |  |
| Who is the sender of the information? |  |
| Who are the potential recipients of the information? |  |
| What principles govern the collection and transmission of this information? |  |

#### Case study: Gaggle, a messaging app for schools

Gaggle is an online platform for classrooms, which replaces tools like blogging
software and email with versions that have content filters. It states that
"Gaggle will not distribute to third parties any staff data or student data
without the consent of either a parent/guardian or a qualified educational
institution except in cases of Possible Student Situations (PSS), which may be
reported to law enforcement." Imagine that a student sent a message to another
student at 8pm on a Saturday, and Gaggle flagged it as a potential sign that the
student is depressed:

| Question | Answer |
| - | - |
| What type of information is shared? | Student data (the message, sender, recipient, timestamp, location) |
| Who is the subject of the information? | The student and their mental health concerns |
| Who is the sender of the information? | The student |
| Who are the potential recipients of the information? | The other student and Gaggle. If Gaggle alerts the parents, school administration, or law enforcement, then they will also become recipients. |
| What principles govern the collection and transmission of this information? | Gaggle's statement above |

The potential recipients can also include _unintended_ recipients (as in many of
our homework assignments), such as people looking over a school administrator's
shoulder, or students and teachers who notice law enforcement being contacted.

## Intro (15 mins)

In this class, we will teach you to follow Value-Sensitive Design. Specifically, this means
for problems that affect people (which, most software does! We rarely build things that do not
impact the world, and thus people, in some way), we follow these steps:

- Identify the stakeholders (i.e., those affected by software)
- Identify the values relevant to the problem
- Fill out a stakeholder-value matrix (i.e., for each stakeholder & value, how do they feel?)
- Identify and analyze conflicts in the matrix (i.e., if two stakeholders feel differently about, e.g., how privacy should be handled in the system, what do you do?)

Sometimes, we will give you certain stakeholders to analyze, or certain values that we
want you to focus on. Other times, it will be up to you to identify one or the
other.

## Class Exercise (40 mins)

Case Study: Algorithmic hiring

Consider this scenario: “Shamazon” (a fictitious company) is looking to hire software engineers, and you have been tasked with designing a tool to filter the submitted resumes and select the ideal candidates for hire.

- Who are the stakeholders?
- What are the values?
- What is in the stakeholder-value matrix?
- What are the conflicts in the matrix? And how can we mitigate them?
- Where are bias and unfairness entering the product? And how can we mitigate them?

## Wrap-up (5 mins)

- Programs are built by people, and affect people, so part of the design process should be understanding the ways that the programs either do or do not match the values of the people affected by them.

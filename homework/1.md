## Project 1: Drawn from Data

:::info[Project 1 has its own repository]

You will work on Project 1 as part of Homeworks 1 and 2. The **work on project 1 needs to be submitted to its own repository**; you can find the Github link to the repository in Pawtograder as part of the Project 1 assignment.

:::

Over Homeworks 1 and 2, you'll find a real dataset, and someone it could help:
people with a question, or a decision, that the data can inform. Then you'll make
them something visual, drawn by your code from the data. For example:

- a field guide to the trees on a street, for a kids' nature walk;
- a buyer's guide to energy-efficient refrigerators, from ENERGY STAR's list of
  certified models, for someone choosing one;
- a guide to the brightest stars in the sky, for a stargazing club;
- a picture of which Bluebikes stations run empty, for the crews who refill
  them;
- trading cards for national parks, for a road-trip game.

What you make, and for whom, is up to you. The pieces below are what every
project needs, whatever it is. Choose something you'd be glad to show someone
outside the course: by the end, you'll have a project of your own to describe.

This homework chooses your audience and data, designs the picture of one record
and a rule for comparing two, and connects them to your data.
[Homework 2](/homework/2) cleans the data, checks your rule against it, and
finishes what you're making.

### Your data

It must be **real data**, recorded about real things: not a dataset made up to
test or demonstrate a library or a tool. If you can't find one, ask course staff
(on Discord, or in person) to help you look. It needs:

- a column that names each record;
- a column of categories, with 3 to 8 values;
- at least three number columns;
- at least 100 rows.

Records must be things, places, or public figures, not private people.

### Required pieces

Most pieces are functions. Where a piece is something else (definitions, or
writing), it says so. Design every function with the whole
[recipe](/class/recipe): its Signature, Docstring, and Examples (in a test
function), and only then its Body. Then list your pieces at the top of
`project.py`, each with the functions or lines that make it up, so that your
panel can find them:

```python skip
# Pieces
# 1. Who it's for: README.md
# 2. A picture of one record: meter, category_color, band, band_badge, icon, record_picture
# 3. The data: lines 80-95
# ...
```

1. **Who it's for.** Writing, in `README.md`: your audience; the question or
   decision you'll help them with; what you'll make for them; and your data,
   with a link to its source, and what each column you use means.
2. **A picture of one record** (Classes 2–5). Functions that together draw one
   record, the unit of what you're making: a card, a scorecard, a guide entry.
   They take numbers and strings, not rows, so you can write them before your
   data is loaded (piece 3), with examples that use values you make up. You
   choose their names and their looks, but you need at least:
   - a function from a number, the largest value that number can have, and a
     color, to an image that shows where the number falls between 0 and the
     largest: a bar, a meter, a stack of shapes;
   - a function from a category to its color, with a branch for each of your
     categories;
   - a function from a number to its band: one of three to five names that mean
     something to your audience (`"short walk"`, `"long walk"`; `"rare"`,
     `"legendary"`), with an example on each boundary; and a function from a
     band to a badge, an image;
   - a function from a category to a small picture of it, drawn from shapes;
   - a function from a record's name, category, and three numbers to the
     picture of the whole record, calling the functions above. Its examples
     compare whole images.
3. **The data** (Classes 7–8). Definitions, not functions: load your CSV with
   `load_table`, and convert each number column you use with
   `transform_column`. Then, in a comment, give each number column's `min`,
   `max`, and `mean`, and say how they made you choose each largest value for
   piece 2. Choose the bands' boundaries from the data, so that each band means
   what its name says, and count each band's records with `filter` and
   `length` to check. Give the counts in the comment.
4. **Pictures from rows** (Class 8):
   - a function from a row to its picture, calling piece 2's;
   - a row function, from a row to `True` or `False`, that picks out the records
     your audience cares about most, used with `filter`;
   - a definition: an image of the three records your audience would look at
     first, side by side.

   Test row functions on rows of a small table of your own, as in Class 8.
5. **A rule** (Classes 5 and 8). Two functions that decide which of two records
   is better for your audience (`"first"`, `"second"`, or `"tie"`): which
   fridge suits a small kitchen, which star is easier to find, which park wins a
   battle. First, define a helper that does the actual comparison, from the
   values it needs (numbers or categories, not rows). Then define the function
   that takes in two rows, and calls the helper with their values. The helper's
   Docstring explains the rule, in two or three sentences, as you would to your
   audience.
6. **Stakeholders** (Class 6). Writing, in comments: a stakeholder matrix for
   what you're making: your audience, and everyone else with a stake in it (the
   people, places, or things in your data, and whoever looks after them), each
   with an interest or value. Then one conflict between two of them, and how
   your design handles it.
7. **A piece of your own.** A function that does something none of pieces 1–6
   do, designed with the whole recipe. For example, it could place a badge in a
   corner of your picture (`overlay_align`, in the [Images](/pll/images)
   reference), break ties in your rule, or add something to your picture from a
   column you haven't used yet. Your project must use it, not just define it,
   and its Docstring says what it does for your audience.

### In the crit

About 15 minutes. Start by showing the three records your audience would look
at first, and your rule deciding between two of them (2 minutes). Then the panel
picks several pieces from your list, and you walk through each in the recipe's
order: what the template is, what the examples show, and what else you could
have done. Then the panel's questions.

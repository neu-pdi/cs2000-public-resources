## Project 1: Drawn from Data

Over Homeworks 1 and 2, you'll find a real dataset, and someone it could help:
people with a question, or a decision, that the data can inform. Then you'll make
them something visual, drawn by your code from the data. For example:

- a field guide to the trees on a street, for a kids' nature walk;
- a buyer's guide to energy-efficient refrigerators, from ENERGY STAR's list of
  certified models, for someone choosing one;
- a guide to the brightest stars in the sky, for a stargazing club;
- a picture of which Bluebikes stations run empty, for the crews who refill
  them;
- trading cards for national parks, for a road-trip game.

What you make, and for whom, is up to you. The pieces below are what every
project needs, whatever it is. Choose something you'd be glad to show someone
outside the course: by the end, you'll have a project of your own to describe.

This homework chooses your audience and data, and designs the picture of one
record and a rule for comparing two. [Homework 2](/homework/2) draws them from
your data's rows, cleans the data, checks your rule against it, and finishes
what you're making.

### Your repository

Project 1 goes in a GitHub repository of its own, which you make now:

1. Go to https://github.com/new, and give the repository a name:
   `cs2000-project1`, or anything you like.
2. Choose **Public** or **Private**: it's up to you. Only you need to see it,
   since you present it yourself, from vscode.dev; but a public repository lets
   you share your project with anyone.
3. Choose to add a README, and click **Create repository**.
4. Type `.` while viewing it, to open it in vscode.dev, as with your
   [scratch repository](/class/2#your-scratch-repository).
5. Make a new file, `project.py`, and type `#level beginner` as its first line.
   (Your scratch repository adds that line to every new file; a new repository
   doesn't.)

Commit and push as you work: at your crit, you present what's in the
repository. [Homework 2](/homework/2) continues in it.

### Your data

Find a dataset, as a CSV file, and put it in your repository: download the
file, and drag it into the Explorer on the left, next to `project.py` (PLL reads
files of up to 50 MB). Keep a link to where you found it, for the comments at
the top of `project.py`.

It must be **real data**, recorded about real things: not a dataset made up to
test or demonstrate a library or a tool. If you can't find one, ask course staff
(on Discord, or in person) to help you look. It needs:

- a column that names each record;
- a column of categories, with around 3 to 8 values (a few more is fine, but less is not);
- at least three number columns;
- at least 100 rows.

Records must be things, places, or public figures, not private people.

### Required pieces

Most pieces are functions. Where a piece is something else (definitions, or
writing), it says so. Design every function with the whole
[recipe](/class/recipe): its Signature, Docstring, and Examples (in test
functions, one per example), and only then its Body. Then list your pieces at
the top of `project.py`, each with the functions or lines that make it up, so
that your panel can find them:

1. **Who it's for.** Describe in English, as a comment (lines starting with `#`) 
   at the top of your `project.py`: your audience; the question or
   decision you'll help them with; what you'll make for them; and your data,
   with a link to its source, and what each column you use means.
2. **A picture of one record** (Classes [2](/class/2)–[5](/class/5)). A set of functions that together draw one
   record, the unit of what you're making: a card, a scorecard, a guide entry, etc.
   These functions take numbers and strings as inputs, not rows, so you can write them before your
   data is loaded (piece 3), with examples that use values you make up. You
   choose the function names and what the images they produce look like, but you need at least:
   - a function of three arguments: a number, the largest value that number can have, and a
     color, to an image that shows where the number falls between 0 and the
     largest: a bar, a meter, a stack of shapes -- your choice depending on what 
     the number represents;
   - a function from a category to its color, with a branch for each of your
     categories;
   - a function from a number to its band (i.e., three to five names that mean
     something to your audience -- `"short walk"`, `"long walk"`; `"rare"`,
     `"legendary"`, etc), with an example on each boundary; 
   - a function from a band to a badge, an image;
   - a function from a category to a small picture of it, drawn from shapes;
   - a function from a record's name, category, and three numbers to the
     picture of the whole record, calling the functions above. Its examples
     compare whole images.
3. **The data** ([Class 7](/class/7)). A series of efinitions, not functions: 
   - load your CSV with `load_table`;
   - convert each number column you use with
     `transform_column` (each can be a separate definition);
   - Then, in a comment, give each number column's `min`,
     `max`, and `mean`, and say how they made you choose each largest value for
     piece 2, and the bands' boundaries, so that each band means what its name
     says.
4. **A rule** (Classes [4](/class/4), [5](/class/5), and [7](/class/7)). A function that decides which of two
   records is better for your audience (`"first"`, `"second"`, or `"tie"`):
   which fridge suits a small kitchen, which star is easier to find, which national park
   wins a battle in a trading card game. It takes the values it needs (numbers or categories, not
   rows), and its Docstring explains the rule, in two or three sentences, as you
   would to your audience. Then, as definitions, use your function on two of your records,
   with values from two rows of your table, and draw both records' pictures.
   ([Homework 2](/homework/2) adds a function that takes the rows themselves, 
   rather than just the numbers, categories, etc.)
5. **Stakeholders** ([Class 6](/class/6)). Writing, in comments: a stakeholder matrix for
   what you're making: your audience, and everyone else with a stake in it (the
   people, places, or things in your data, and whoever looks after them), each
   with an interest or value. Then one conflict between two of them, and how
   your design handles it.
6. **A piece of your own.** One function, designed with the whole recipe, in one
   of these shapes — whichever your project needs:
   - **a new value**, computed from a record, shown in its picture (a rank, a
     ratio, how far off the best it is);
   - **a new choice** in the rule of piece 4: a tie-break, or a factor from a
     column you haven't used yet;
   - **a new view** of a record's picture: an extra mark, badge, or bar that no
     other piece draws (`overlay_align`, in the [Images](/pll/images)
     reference).

   Whichever you pick, it hangs off the function that draws a record's picture,
   or off your rule, so your project calls it rather than just defining it. Its
   Docstring says what it does for your audience.

### In the crit

About 15 minutes. Start by showing two of your records' pictures, and your rule
deciding between them (2 minutes). Then the panel
picks several pieces from your list, and you walk through the code following the recipe: 
what the data is, what the examples show, and what else you could
have done. Panel's can interrupt with questions at any time.

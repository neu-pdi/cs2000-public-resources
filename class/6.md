## Notes (review before class)
_Adapted from the CS 2100 lecture notes on ethics: [stakeholder-value matrices](https://neu-pdi.github.io/cs2100-public-resources/lecture-notes/next/l9-ethics), and [bias and privacy](https://neu-pdi.github.io/cs2100-public-resources/lecture-notes/next/l10-ethics)._

### Why Value-Sensitive Design?

- Computer science is a group activity: this course gives you the vocabulary of
  moral language, to talk with teammates and advocate for what is right.
- **Value-Sensitive Design** helps us identify and defend privacy issues, against
  "I have nothing to hide": its **stakeholder-value matrices** show who is at risk
  because they _do_ "have something to hide."
- It comes first, with the requirements: it shapes what a program should do,
  before any code or data. Without it, other values take over:
  - Social media was built to connect communities, but its company (also a
    stakeholder) earns more the longer users look, at the cost of their mental health.
  - reCAPTCHA (verifying users are human) and Duolingo (teaching languages) also
    train AI on users' data; and Duolingo's finances favor viewing over learning.

### Steps for Value-Sensitive Design

1. **Identify the stakeholders**: the people affected by the software, in any way.
2. **Identify the values** at stake for those stakeholders.
3. **Fill out a stakeholder-value matrix**: a row for each stakeholder, a column for each value, and in each cell, how that stakeholder's value relates to the software.
4. **Identify and analyze conflicts in the matrix**: for each, decide whether and how to address it.

### Case Study: Finder, a Dating App

**Step 1: stakeholders** of "Finder," a fictitious dating app:

- Users, who are many groups: nonbinary and transgender people, people who date
  all genders, people whose sexual orientation is criminalized where they live,
  people not legally allowed where they live, sex workers, survivors of intimate
  partner violence, blind people, non-English speakers, people with apostrophes
  in their names, ...
- Beyond users: **content moderators** (exposed to harmful content), **software
  engineers** and other **employees**, **advertisers**, **actors and influencers**
  in ads, **governments and regulators**, and **anyone who dates** (apps changed
  dating for everyone), and their friends and families. More will appear over time.

**Step 2: values.** Values shape technology, acknowledged or not, and technology
changes our practices and values in turn.

- **Privacy** (key in this course): hiding one's identity from a government, or
  location from an abuser. **Safety** can be separate, or part of privacy.
- **Accessibility** differs by user: a nonbinary gender option; screen readers
  and translation apps; entering one's actual name.
- **Fairness** only happens if prioritized: recommendations should use only
  users' own preferences.
- Also **financial cost** (to advertisers and users), **reputation**, and **laws
  and regulations**.

**Step 3: the matrix** (part of it):

| | Privacy | Accessibility | Financial cost | Laws and regulations |
| -- | -- | -- | -- | -- |
| People whose sexual orientation is criminalized where they live | Want to hide their orientation from the government | Need to search profiles of appropriate genders from their location | | |
| Survivors of intimate partner violence | Want to hide their location from certain people | Need two-factor authentication, to prevent account takeover | | Hope we go beyond the minimum regulations |
| Advertisers | Have some data to share, some to keep private | Want users' contact information, but shouldn't get it | Want to reach more people for less money | Must follow advertising regulations |
| Governments and regulators | Want their identities hidden from users | Need access to what they need, and nothing more | Need to collect accurate taxes | Must enforce the law |

**Step 4: conflicts.**

- Advertisers' low costs push costs onto users or the company: a financial model
  chooses between them.
- Many users want their identities or locations private from the government, which
  may want them to enforce laws. Mitigations: share nothing without a subpoena,
  or only specific information (never location or sexual orientation).
- Fairness and accessibility can risk Finder's reputation, depending on whose
  opinions count; and fairness can require collecting personal data, compromising
  privacy.
- Criminalized groups simply want to exist legally and peacefully, while
  governments target them.

### Throughout the Process: Bias and Unfairness

Bias and unfairness are built into society: countering them is continuous, and
doesn't end with a matrix. They **enter** technology through:

- unrepresentative data ("the general population," leaving out women);
- biased collection (a survey where no choice fits some people);
- builders misunderstanding the data's context, or making mistakes (correlation
  taken for causation);
- use for another purpose (a platform for vital communication in vulnerable
  communities, used to harass them).

They are **created or amplified** in use, as technology:

- reflects compounding historical injustices (the numbers computers use for words
  carry the biases of their source text);
- magnifies users' implicit biases and stereotypes;
- helps one population, but harms or excludes another;
- feeds back on itself (social media boosts posts that already have attention).

### Privacy

- **Privacy**: "the ability to determine for ourselves when, how, and to what
  extent information about us is communicated to others" (Westin, 1967).
- Seen _only_ as the individual against society or the state (the supposed
  privacy vs. national security "tradeoff"), privacy often loses. So we judge it
  in its social context, with five questions (the left column below).

**Case study: Gaggle**, a classroom platform with content-filtered blogs and
email. A student messages another at 8pm on a Saturday, and Gaggle flags it as a
possible sign of depression:

| Question | Answer |
| - | - |
| What type of information is shared? | Student data (the message, sender, recipient, timestamp, location) |
| Who is the subject of the information? | The student and their mental health concerns |
| Who is the sender of the information? | The student |
| Who are the potential recipients of the information? | The other student and Gaggle; and if Gaggle alerts them, parents, school administration, or law enforcement |
| What principles govern the collection and transmission of this information? | Gaggle's policy: no student or staff data goes to third parties without consent from a parent/guardian or the school, except in "Possible Student Situations," which may be reported to law enforcement |

Recipients include _unintended_ ones (as in many homework assignments): someone
looking over an administrator's shoulder; students and teachers who notice law
enforcement being contacted.

### A Complete Example: Zoomr Scooters

A city lets "Zoomr" (a fictitious company) put electric scooters for rent on its
sidewalks. Riders unlock a scooter with Zoomr's app (which charges by the minute,
and records each trip's route), and leave it anywhere when they are done. Here is
the whole analysis, every step: the matrix's rows are the stakeholders (step 1),
and its columns are the values (step 2).

| | Safety | Accessibility | Privacy | Financial cost |
| -- | -- | -- | -- | -- |
| Riders | Want working brakes and lights, and safe places to ride | Need an app that works with screen readers, in many languages | Don't want their trips (from home, to work, to a clinic) tracked or sold | Want short trips to be cheap |
| Pedestrians who use wheelchairs or are blind | Don't want to trip over, or be hit by, scooters | Need sidewalks and curb ramps kept clear | | |
| People without a smartphone or bank card | | Can't rent a scooter at all, without another way to unlock and pay | | |
| "Chargers" (gig workers paid to charge scooters overnight) | Work alone at night, lifting heavy scooters | | The app tracks their location while they work | Want fair pay for each scooter they charge |
| Zoomr | Wants few injuries, to avoid lawsuits | | Trip data shows where to put scooters, and can be sold | Earns more when scooters are everywhere, and riders can leave them anywhere |
| The city | Responsible for safe streets and sidewalks | Must follow disability law | Wants trip data, to plan bike lanes | Wants fees from Zoomr, and not to pay for cleanup |

**Conflicts** (step 4):

1. Zoomr earns more when riders can leave scooters anywhere, but scooters left on
   sidewalks and curb ramps block pedestrians who use wheelchairs or are blind.
   _Mitigation_: a trip can only end in a marked parking zone, so the app keeps
   charging until the rider parks in one.
2. The city wants trip data, but where a trip starts and ends can reveal where a
   rider lives, works, or gets medical care. _Mitigation_: Zoomr gives the city
   only the number of trips on each street each day, never individual trips.

**Bias**: Zoomr puts the most scooters where the most trips started last month,
so a neighborhood that starts with few scooters has few trips, and keeps getting
few scooters: a feedback loop.

Try:

- Which stakeholders in the matrix never ride a scooter? Which cells show why
  they belong in it anyway?
- Mitigation 1 resolves conflict 1. Which cells of the matrix show who pays for it?
- For the trip data in conflict 2: who is the subject, who is the sender, and who
  are the potential recipients (before and after the mitigation)?

## Live

Each of you gets one of two scenarios (your neighbor has the other) and does a
stakeholder analysis of it on paper: the stakeholders, the values, the matrix,
a conflict with a mitigation, and where bias could enter. Then you present your
analysis to your neighbor, who questions it, and switch: practice for the crits
in lab.

## Practice

Case Study: Algorithmic hiring

Consider this scenario: “Shamazon” (a fictitious company) is looking to hire software engineers, and you have been tasked with designing a tool to filter the submitted resumes and select the ideal candidates for hire.

- Who are the stakeholders?
- What are the values?
- What is in the stakeholder-value matrix?
- What are the conflicts in the matrix? And how can we mitigate them?
- Where are bias and unfairness entering the product? And how can we mitigate them?

Case Study: Emoji design

We use emojis to represent ourselves and our thoughts; the people who design
them have to decide what an emoji looks like, and which ones there are.

- Two values in emoji design are **simplicity** and **representation**. For
  each, name a stakeholder who holds it. (Not an emoji design: who would want
  emojis to be simple, and who would want them to be representative?)
- In a sentence or two: how can the two come into conflict? Why is it hard to
  make emojis that are both simple and representative?

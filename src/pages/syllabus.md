## Course Description

Introduces computer science and data science to students with no prior programming experience. Starts by building programs with numbers, text, and images. Explores real, complex datasets both interactively and through coding by doing data analysis, constructing graphs, and computing interesting results. Offers students an opportunity to build a solid foundation and practice coding with a popular industrial coding language with a professional programmer’s interface to the code. Covers skills needed to identify and respond to ethical challenges that arise in the program design process.

### Course Structure

There will be three in-person **classes** and one **lab** per week. All content is directed around teaching a set of 13 **skills**, assessment of which will form the primary assessment of the class, and which are described in detail at [Skills](/skills).

There are [13 **skills**](/syllabus#skills), which have repeatable assessments (taken on paper without notes over the course of the semester), and form the primary grading for the class. 

There are no formal quizzes or exams in this class, though the skill assessments are similar in form to quizzes; unlike traditional quizzes, skill assessments allow you to be assessed at your own pace, and repeat as you need.

#### Classes

Each class period (of CS2000) has a page, in three parts. Its **Notes**, at the top, you are **expected to review _before_ class** (only Class 1's are read after it): class time doesn't repeat them, but builds on them. Its **Live** section says briefly what happens in class, which changes from day to day: for example, worksheets on paper, alone or with a partner; tracing programs by hand; designing a function in turns with a small group; practice crits; short lectures on tricky ideas; and predicting what code will do, then arguing for your answer. Most of the time, you will be working with classmates, assisted by your instructor. Its **Practice** is for after class: more problems on that day's material, to do on your own (the [Skills](/skills) page lists the Practice that goes with each skill).

#### Homeworks

There will also be **homework** assignments, due every two weeks. Each homework is half of one of three open-ended projects, which you design, for an audience you choose: [Project 1](/homework/1) is Homeworks 1 and 2, [Project 2](/homework/3) is Homeworks 3 and 4, and [Project 3](/homework/5) is Homeworks 5 and 6. Each homework lists the **required pieces** your project must have, which practice the skills used for the **skill assessments**. Homeworks are the subject of [Assignment Crits](/syllabus#assignment-crits) in lab, which are the only assessment that is done on them. 

#### Labs

The lab period (CS2001) alternates between two kinds of lab, as the [calendar](/) shows:

- **Practice labs** ([Lab 0](/lab/0) to Lab 6, each with its own page) are for pair programming, on problems from the classes since the last practice lab. You work in pairs, with one laptop open: the **driver** types, and the **copilot** reads ahead, suggests what to try, and catches mistakes, and you switch roles at each problem. This is how your [Pair Programming Sessions](#pair-programming-sessions) with a TA work, with you as the driver. Staff take attendance, and participating in practice labs can raise your grade (see [Grading](#grading)).
- **Crit labs** are for [Assignment Crits](/syllabus#assignment-crits): you present your homework, which is due then, to a panel of classmates, and are on the panel for theirs. Grading of Assignment Crits forms part of your final grade, and you must be physically present to participate. 

Students can register for any CS 2001 lab section that is on their campus with available seats! Students must stick to the lab they registered for and cannot go to other labs if they miss their scheduled one.

### Assignment Crits {#assignment-crits}

Every other week's lab is for crits: presentations of the homework due then. The lab sits in panels of 5 or 6 students, with TAs moving between them, and everyone on a panel takes a turn presenting. The second lab of the semester introduces how crits work (and Class 7 has a practice crit); the first crit, on Homework 1, is two weeks after it.

A crit is one presentation, about 15 minutes including questions. The presenter starts it in vscode.dev, with the Assignment Crits extension, and reads out its join code; the panel joins in the browser, on their own laptops, and follows the presenter's code as they walk through their work: what they did, why they did it, and what else they could have done. Panelists raise a hand (in the browser) to ask a question aloud, and the presenter takes the questions in order. Every five minutes, and once at the end, each panelist rates the presentation, with one tap; when a question has been answered, the rest of the panel rates the question too. No one sees anyone else's ratings.

A presentation's score is the mean of the panel's ratings, each weighted by how careful that rater has been over the semester so far (roughly: how well their ratings agree with everyone else's), and every score shows how reliable it is. Your grade for a lab has two parts: a score on your work (your presentation and the questions you asked) and a second score on the ratings you gave (and how closely they tracked with others). Staff mark who is present: nothing done by someone not marked present counts, so coming late or leaving early may result in no credit on the crit. 

### Pair Programming Sessions

Working with others is a fundamentally important skill, and one of the best ways to learn is to work or supervised by someone who is more experienced than you. As a result, over the course of the semester you will have **three 1hr pair programming sessions with a TA**. These will be scheduled in the first, middle, and last third of the class, beginning a few weeks into the semester. You will have the opportunity to pick a time that is convenient to you, and will work with whomever of the TAs is available at the selected time. You can sign up on your own, or, if you'd like, together with a classmate: two students and one TA. If you want to work with anothers 

The sessions will take place in rooms with several TAs and their students, and each session is given a problem that is new to everyone in it, from a pool of problems appropriate at that point in the semester (beginning, middle, end). The material covered will only be up to the _beginning_ of the phase (consult the semester calendar). Only one computer is open: the student is the driver/pilot, whereas the TA serves as the copilot, helping to brainstorm, point out typos, etc. If you signed up with a classmate, you take turns: one of you drives while the other is a copilot alongside the TA, and you switch roles at each part of the problem. While the problems are intended to take roughly an hour to complete, grading of these sessions is not based on functional completeness of the solution, but rather, based on the quality of interaction — working together, coming up with ideas, demonstrating familiarity with the course material, etc. In a session with a classmate, each of you is graded on your own part in it. ### Grading

Your final grade will be calculated by your results in the **Skill Assessments**, **Assignment Crits**, and **Pair Programming** sessions, as described above, with a bonus for attending **practice labs** (below the table) and **class attendance** (above Assignment Crits & Pairing Sessions). The exact grade associated with performance in each of those categories is captured by the following table — the highest row that a student satisfies all columns of will be their grade, and **if no rows are completely satisfied, the student will not pass the course.**

Because each of the three pairing sessions is worth a third of the **Pairing Session Average**, and a missed session scores 0 (see [Pair Programming Sessions](#pair-programming-sessions)), the thresholds in the last column are set around what missing a session costs: with full credit on the sessions you do attend, one miss averages 66.7%, two misses 33.3%, and three misses 0. So one missed session still clears the 65% needed for an **A-** but not the 70% needed for an **A**; two missed sessions leave you at a **C+**; missing all three leaves that column short of every row from A through C-, so the highest row you can still reach is **D+**, which asks for nothing there.

### Collaboration and Academic Honesty

Computer science, both academically and professionally, is a collaborative discipline. In any collaboration, however, all parties are expected to make their own contributions and to generously credit the contributions of others.

Specifically:

- You are responsible for any material you turn in. It is vitally important that you turn in work that you understand.
- Copying material from another person with or without their knowledge is not allowed.
- Any sharing or receiving information about the content of skill assessments is an academic integrity violation, and may result in failing the class.
- Attempting the same skill assessment variant twice without notifying the proctor of the error is an academic integrity violation.
- Trying to access any electronics during skill assessments is an academic integrity violation and will result in a 0 on that skill. This includes having a phone in your lap, no matter what is accessed on the phone. You will also be prohibited from re-attempting said skill.

You _should_ _write_ all code for projects yourself. Even if you discuss solutions, or approaches, with others, do the actual typing on your own! And avoid playing games or trying to find loopholes — i.e., do not merely type what someone says, or type what you see on another's screen. While we do not consider collaboration a violation, relying on others for your solutions may result in you not learning the material, and in this class, not learning the material will result in not being able to pass the skill assessments. 

Remember, your projects are not graded directly, rather, it is the basis of your crit, where you present it and answer questions about it — if you do not understand what you present, you will fail that assessment, nevermind that without the practice of working on your project, you will find it difficult to pass the skill assessments that form the core of your grade in the course (consult the [grading table](#grading)), so take projects for what they are intended: extensive opportunity to practice what we are teaching, plus the opportunity to practice talking about your work.

The minimum penalty for an academic integrity violation is a zero on that aspect of the grade and a report to the [Office of Student Conduct and Conflict Resolution (OSCCR)](https://osccr.sites.northeastern.edu/). Penalties are increased if there are aggravating factors, such as stealing another student's work or lying about cheating. Also, see the [Official University Academic Integrity Policy](https://osccr.sites.northeastern.edu/academic-integrity-policy/).

### The AI Policy

AI coding assistants like Claude, Copilot, or Cursor should not be used in this course. Producing project solutions with these tools will both prevent you from learning the techniques needed for the skill assessments, and it will make doing your assignment crits much more difficult.Until you have the ability to design, understand, and review code, using an AI assistant amounts to wandering around in the dark, with no ability to determine if you are getting closer to what you want. 

The same is true of asking questions of chat models like ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, etc. Putting assignment instructions into such a model and getting out code or test cases amounts to getting the model to do the thinking for you — and as a result, you will not learn. "Only looking" at AI-generated solutions "before writing your own" undermines the learning just the same, as you will not be learning to actually solve problems, you will just be copying. It will also backfire, as if you do this, you will find yourself unable to present your work, and answer questions about it, in the crits that form the grade for these assignments.

### Inclusive Environment

To create and preserve a classroom atmosphere that optimizes teaching and learning, all participants share a responsibility in creating a civil and non-disruptive forum for the discussion of ideas. Students are expected to conduct themselves at all times in a manner that does not disrupt teaching or learning. Your comments to others should be constructive and free from harassing statements. You are encouraged to disagree with other students, TAs, and the instructor, but such disagreements need to be respectful and be based upon facts and documentation (rather than prejudices and personalities). The course staff reserves the right to interrupt conversations that deviate from these expectations or cause any sort of disruption of learning for others. Repeated unprofessional or disrespectful conduct, including ignoring requests to cease disruptive behavior, may result in a lower grade or more severe consequences. Part of the learning process in this course is respectful engagement of ideas with others.

We believe that diversity and inclusiveness are essential to excellence in academic discourse and innovation. In this class, the perspective of people of all races, ethnicities, gender expressions and gender identities, religions, sexual orientations, disabilities, socioeconomic backgrounds, and nationalities will be respected and viewed as a resource and benefit throughout the semester. Suggestions to further diversify class materials and assignments are encouraged. If any course meetings conflict with your religious events, please do not hesitate to reach out to Amy Gately (a.gately@northeastern.edu) to make alternative arrangements.

### Title IX

Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 protects individuals from sex or gender-based discrimination, including discrimination based on gender-identity, in educational programs and activities that receive federal financial assistance.

If you or someone you know has been a survivor of a Prohibited Offense, confidential support and guidance can be found through University Health and Counseling Services and the Center for Spiritual Dialogue and Service clergy members.

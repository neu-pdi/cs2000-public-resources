## A Phone Tree

Some teams spread news with a phone tree: the first person calls two others,
each of them calls two more, and so on.

```python
@dataclass
class NoOne:
    pass

@dataclass
class Caller:
    name: str
    team: str
    joined: int
    first: "PhoneTree"
    second: "PhoneTree"

# A PhoneTree is who gets a call: no one, or a Caller, who joined in the year
# `joined`, and then calls the two PhoneTrees `first` and `second`.
PhoneTree = NoOne | Caller

kai = Caller("Kai", "red", 2021, NoOne(), NoOne())
lena = Caller("Lena", "blue", 2019, NoOne(), NoOne())
mo = Caller("Mo", "red", 2023, kai, lena)
nia = Caller("Nia", "blue", 2024, NoOne(), NoOne())
oren = Caller("Oren", "red", 2018, mo, nia)
```

## Problem 1

Write `_phone_tree_temp`. Then design `count_team`, which gives how many people
in a phone tree are on a given team.

## Problem 2

Design `depth`, the number of people in the longest chain of calls: `oren`'s is
3 (Oren, Mo, and Kai or Lena), and a `NoOne()`'s is 0.

## Problem 3

Design `earliest_joined`, the year in which the longest-standing person in a
phone tree joined. For a `NoOne()`, give 2050, a year in which no one has joined
yet.

## Problem 4

Design `names_starting_with`, which takes a phone tree and a letter, and gives
the names in the tree that start with that letter, upper or lower case.

## Problem 5

In some families -- maybe including yours -- people want to be able to
understand how they're related to others in their family. Family trees can be
useful for that purpose. They represent familial relationships in certain ways.
For example, a node might represent an ancestor, and a child represents ...
well, a child or a descendant.

Often, but not always, family trees show both (a) legally-recognized
relationships and (b) relationships in which the children are directly
genetically related to their parents. That means that people who don't meet both
conditions (a) and (b) might not be included in a family tree.

Deciding whom to put in a family tree has emotional and psychological
consequences; it also reflects political and legal stakes. For example:
determining citizenship (who can apply for residency or citizenship based on
family relationships?), finances (whose debts are you responsible for paying?),
medical decision-making authority (what decisions can someone make on someone
else's behalf?), and inheritance (who will automatically inherit if there is no
will?) all depend on definitions of who counts as "family" or "relatives". And
in many places, ideas about what a family is, and about which **familial
relations** should be openly acknowledged and documented, are changing.

### Part A

If you had to design a data definition for a family tree, then what kinds of
relations do you think it should represent? In other words, what counts as
"being part of the same family"? Please include a justification of your answer
in two or three sentences.

### Part B

Given your answer to the previous question, what would you name the nodes and
the children in your tree? Explain your answer in two or three sentences.
